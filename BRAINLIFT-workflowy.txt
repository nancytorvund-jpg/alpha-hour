BrainLift: Alpha Hours
	Owner
		Nancy Wisniewski Torvund, proposed Head of Alpha Hours
	Purpose
		Primary purpose
			Design and run Alpha Hours: a premium after-school program that delivers the full Alpha academic day on Timeback to K-8 students who do not attend Alpha. Families attend four hours a week: a two-night track (Monday/Wednesday or Tuesday/Thursday, 5:00 to 7:00 pm) or a single four-hour Saturday block, all inside existing Alpha campuses. It covers both subjects (Math and Reading/Writing) every sitting, and enrolls families one seven-week session at a time, five sessions a year.
			The program exists to do three things at once
				Generate profit from a premium after-school offering priced above every competitor.
				Use real estate we already pay for, turning empty evening and weekend hours in Alpha buildings into revenue.
				Feed Alpha. Give families a low-friction, paid way to try the Alpha model, and convert a share of them into full-time enrollment.
				Success is measured on all three: site profit, building utilization, and conversions to full Alpha.
		Out of scope
			Custom changes to Timeback for after-school use. The program runs the platform as built.
			Certified-teacher tutoring models or one-on-one private tutoring.
			Test-prep-only offerings (SAT and ACT).
			Non-academic enrichment (arts, sports, social clubs) as the core product.
			Full-day or full-time schooling. This is strictly after-school and Saturday.
			Online-only delivery. The program is in-person inside Alpha buildings.
			Childcare or custodial care. Alpha Hours is academic instruction, structured to qualify as instruction under state exemptions.
			Single-subject enrollment. Both subjects are always included. Alpha Hours is one product, not a menu.
			Subjects beyond Math and Reading/Writing, or grades outside K-8, at launch.
	Experts
		MacKenzie Price, co-founder of Alpha School
			Co-founded Alpha in Austin in 2014 and is the public voice of the model. Built a private-school network that puts core academics on an AI platform for about two hours a day, growing from about 200 students to over 1,000 in two years across a fast-expanding campus network.
			Why follow: She proved the Alpha model draws families at $40,000 to $75,000 tuition and that parents will pay for measurable results delivered by software plus guides, not teachers. Alpha Hours is her model, unbundled into the after-school hours.
		Toru Kumon, founder of Kumon
			Built one of the largest after-school brands in history, 25,000+ centers, on a simple idea: master before you progress, and make progress visible to parents.
			Why follow: He showed a tightly standardized, low-touch mastery system scales globally without certified teachers. Kumon is the volume baseline Alpha Hours must beat on outcomes and price.
		Larry Martinek, co-founder of Mathnasium
			Turned classroom math methods into a franchise built on trained non-teacher instructors and a fixed method.
			Why follow: His model proves the market pays a premium for structured help delivered by trained guides rather than credentialed teachers, which is exactly Alpha Hours' staffing thesis.
		Raymond and Eileen Huntington, founders of Huntington Learning Center
			Built one of the first broad-subject tutoring franchises, anchored on diagnostic assessment and structured programs.
			Why follow: They showed assessment data builds trust and justifies higher prices. Alpha Hours replaces their diagnostics with continuous Timeback mastery data, which is stronger proof delivered more often.
		Michelle Rose Gilman, founder of Fusion Academy
			Grew a garage tutoring program into a one-to-one private school charging roughly $8,000 to $17,000 per course per year.
			Why follow: Fusion is the current price ceiling for structured academic programs below full private school, and the reference class Alpha Hours should be compared against, not Kumon.
		Command Education and Lindamood-Bell (premium-pricing reference)
			Command Education charges up to $750,000 per package for college consulting. Lindamood-Bell charges over $10,000 for a six-week reading intensive.
			Why follow: Both price against the value of the outcome, not hours delivered. They are the template for Alpha Hours' pricing logic: anchor on the result, quote the annual number, keep the per-hour math for objections.
	Spiky Points of View
		Truths
			Truth 1: Your child gets Alpha's entire academic day here, and it beats the six hours they sat through this morning.
				Alpha's own data claims about 2x learning in roughly two hours a day. Alpha Hours does not sample that, it delivers the whole dose: the same two hours Alpha's own students run, either as two focused evenings or one Saturday sitting. Every family who pays for Alpha Hours is running an experiment that indicts the school they already write checks to. We do not really compete with Kumon. We compete with the six hours their child sat through earlier that day, and we win.
			Truth 2: Certified teachers are a downgrade, and removing them is the product, not a cost cut.
				The industry sells "certified teacher" as the quality floor. In a mastery system it is the ceiling. A teacher paces the whole room to the middle, brings good days and bad days, and cannot personalize past a handful of kids. The software has none of those limits. So we hire non-teachers, pay them well, and forbid them from teaching. The absence of the teacher is not something we tolerate to save money. It is the reason the results are better.
			Truth 3: We price to exclude, on purpose.
				Alpha Hours is not democratizing education and will not pretend to. The price is a filter. It is set high enough to select families who can later sign a $65,000 tuition contract, because those are the families the program exists to find. Price it lower and you fill the room with families who will never convert and drown the guides in low-value seats. The exclusivity is not a regrettable side effect of a premium price. It is the entire job of the price.
			Truth 4: This is a real-estate business that happens to teach children.
				The asset is the building. Alpha already controls it, owned or leased, and it sits dark from late afternoon and on weekends. Alpha Hours is the arbitrage that monetizes the idle hours, and every decision should be judged on yield per campus operating day. The academics have to be genuinely excellent, but they are the product we sell, not the reason the business exists. The reason is that empty premium real estate in the country's richest zip codes is the most underused asset Alpha holds, and we are the ones turning the lights back on.
			Truth 5: Alpha Hours is bait, and we should say so out loud, at least to ourselves.
				Dressed as an after-school program, it is a customer-acquisition weapon pointed at families who are currently enrolled somewhere else. Its most valuable output is not the tuition it collects, it is the families it peels off competing schools and hands to full Alpha, each worth up to $325,000 across their years. Treating it as "just enrichment" would waste it. Build the conversion handoff as if the whole point depends on it, because it does.
			Truth 6: Adult help is noise. The best thing a grown-up can do for a child's academics is get out of the way.
				Parents, tutors, and teachers all believe their involvement is the value. Inside a mastery loop it is interference. Every time an adult explains the answer, rescues the child, or sets the pace, they break the feedback loop the software runs on and train dependence instead of mastery. The guide's real skill is restraint: hold effort and motivation, and refuse to teach. We are selling parents a program whose core discipline is that no adult, including them, gets to interrupt the algorithm.
			Truth 7: Grades, homework, and the 180-day calendar are administrative fictions, and we bill against all three.
				One room holds a kindergartner and a seventh grader on the same platform and neither slows the other, which means grade-level cohorts were never about learning. There is no homework, because the hours are the work. The year is five seven-week sessions a family buys one at a time, because a child should be able to purchase exactly as much acceleration as they want. Every convention of school we throw out turns out to have been about running the institution, not teaching the child.
			Truth 8: There is no ceiling, because the constraint is buildings and no one builds them faster than Alpha.
				Every full-capacity number is a floor. The entire network at capacity still captures under 1 percent of the premium families in the metros Alpha already sits in. Alpha Hours is not fighting for share in a mature market, it is limited only by how many campuses exist, and that count climbs about 20 a year. The company that wins this is whoever already owns the most buildings in the most expensive neighborhoods in America. That is Alpha, so the runway is the map of the country.
		Myths
			Myth 1: After-school should be affordable.
				Affordable is a race to the bottom that signals low value and attracts families who will argue over $200 a month. We win by being the single most expensive option in the after-school market, because in premium education the price is read as proof. The goal is not to be within everyone's reach. It is to be the program every other program gets measured against and cannot match.
			Myth 2: Parents are buying their child's happiness.
				At this price parents buy certainty, not smiles. They will tolerate a demanding, two-evenings-a-week program for as long as the mastery data climbs, and they will abandon a fun one the moment it stops producing. Enjoyment matters only because an engaged child produces results faster. Sell the proof, not the fun, and never confuse the two.
			Myth 3: A good trial lowers the barrier to entry.
				Free, easy trials attract tire-kickers who never buy. Alpha Hours is the highest-commitment trial in education, five figures and two evenings a week for weeks on end, and that is precisely why it converts. The size of the commitment is the qualifier. A family willing to do this has already told you they will do full Alpha.
			Myth 4: Teaching two subjects at once needs two specialists.
				One non-teacher runs Math and Reading for a room of mixed grades at the same time, because subject expertise in the adult is irrelevant when the platform holds all of it. The specialist model is not rigor, it is overhead that competitors are trapped paying and passing on to parents. We deleted the specialist and kept the outcome.
			Myth 5: You cannot charge Fusion prices without Fusion's one-to-one staffing.
				We charge premium prices at one guide per ten students, because the personalization lives in the software, not in the ratio. Fusion's 1:1 model is not a quality advantage, it is a cost structure that caps how large and how profitable it can ever become. We took the personalization and left their cost behind.
			Myth 6: An empty school building is a cost to manage.
				An empty premium school building after 5:00 pm is the most valuable idle asset Alpha controls, owned or leased, and every school in the country that lets its buildings go dark is setting money on fire for no reason. The building is already paid for. The only real question is whether you are operator enough to run a second, higher-margin business inside it while the competition goes home.
	Insights
		Insight 1: The unit of the business is the campus operating day, not the student.
			Facility, coordinator, and security costs are per-day and fixed. Software and guide costs are per-student and variable. Profit comes from loading as many students as safely possible onto each fixed campus day, which is why filling all four cohorts matters more than squeezing price.
		Insight 2: Price against Fusion and Lindamood-Bell, never against Kumon.
			Anchoring the family's comparison determines whether $4,500 a session reads as expensive or as a bargain. Framed against a Fusion course term, a Lindamood-Bell intensive, and one-third-of-Alpha for the full year, it reads as obvious value. Marketing controls the anchor.
		Insight 3: Blend the guide role across day and evening to make it hireable.
			A 20-hour evenings-and-Saturday job has chronic turnover. A daytime-plus-evening role at $50,000 to $85,000 is a real career, attracts stronger people, and shares the guide's cost with the campus. The staffing model is the hidden make-or-break of a nationwide rollout.
		Insight 4: Design as instruction, not care, from day one.
			Whether the state calls an evening program childcare or exempt instruction decides the entire regulatory burden. Structuring it as a class with a curriculum, avoiding meals where they trigger licensing, and signing a formal host-campus agreement keeps the program exempt in most states. Every block is a curriculum-driven instructional period; the Saturday four-hour block is structured as two two-hour instructional periods with a supervised break, and no lunch is served. Flag the Saturday format specifically in each state's written exemption request.
		Insight 5: Build the conversion handoff on purpose, or the feeder does not fire.
			Camp-to-school research is clear: conversion only happens when the school builds an intentional enrollment process. Alpha Hours must end every family's experience with a structured conversation, using the child's own Timeback data as the evidence, or the largest source of value goes uncollected.
		Insight 6: The standalone business and the feeder are two separate wins, and both are real.
			Alpha Hours is a profitable, standalone after-school business on its own numbers, before counting a single conversion. Present it that way first, so it never depends on the feeder to justify itself. Then show the feeder on top as the strategic multiplier. Two independent reasons to say yes are stronger than one.
	Knowledge Tree
		Category 1: How non-school learning environments drive academic gains
			Summary: Mastery pedagogy, standardized pacing, and visible progress let after-school programs outperform classrooms, using process-driven personalization rather than teacher variation.
			Kumon: Founded 1954. Worksheet-based mastery, non-certified staff, 25,000+ centers, $150 to $220 per subject per month. Personalization lives in the process, not the content.
			Mathnasium: Founded 2002. Trained non-teacher instructors, fixed method, math only, $250 to $500 per month.
			Alpha / Timeback: Founded 2014. Core academics on an adaptive AI platform in about two hours a day; guides, not teachers; rebranded from 2 Hour Learning to Timeback in November 2025. Alpha's 2x to 2.6x speed and top 1-2% MAP claims are internally measured, not independently audited.
			Fusion Academy: One-to-one private school, $8,000 to $17,000 per course per year. Proves premium willingness to pay for personalized academics.
			Insights: Mastery scales when the system, not the teacher, carries instruction. Parents validate trust through visible, routine progress. Premium after-school is about quality of result, not quantity of hours.
		Category 2: After-school business models and the feeder
			Summary: After-school programs scale through standardization, real-estate leverage, and, for a school-owned program, conversion to full enrollment.
			Market size: US tutoring $18.9B, growing ~11%/yr; after-school $21.0B with 22.6M children unable to get a spot; ~36M K-8 students; parent willingness to pay ~$357/month.
			Huntington: Founded 1977. Diagnostic-driven, multi-subject, $40 to $75 per hour. Assessment builds trust and justifies price.
			Premium pricing precedent: Command Education $120,000+; Lindamood-Bell $10,000+ for six weeks; Fusion $60,000+ full-time. All price against outcome value, not hours.
			Feeder economics: Paid, high-engagement trials convert at 25%+ in education; one enrollment is worth $100,000+ in lifetime tuition; private-school marketing costs $3,700 to $5,800 per enrolled student. Alpha Hours inverts this because the prospect pays during consideration.
			Insights: Standardization plus assessment builds scalable trust. Real estate already controlled is the margin. For a school-owned program, the feeder is the largest source of value, and it only fires with an intentional handoff.
	Business Model and Operations
		The program
			A normal Alpha day gives students about two hours of core academics on Timeback. Alpha Hours delivers that same two-hour academic day, after school. A family picks a two-night track, Monday/Wednesday or Tuesday/Thursday at 5:00 to 7:00 pm, or a single Saturday block of four hours, morning or afternoon. Either way the child gets four hours a week on the platform, the full Alpha academic dose, roughly half on Math and half on Reading/Writing, adjusting to wherever they need the time. The software delivers the instruction and adapts to each child. The guide manages focus, effort, and motivation. That is the whole model: the complete Alpha academic day, mastery-based, four hours a week.
		Schedule and tracks
			Runs Monday through Thursday evenings and Saturday, inside existing Alpha campuses.
			Weekday evening schedule
				Setup: 4:45 to 5:00 pm (guides prepare, devices ready).
				Block: 5:00 to 7:00 pm.
				Dismissal and reset: 7:00 to 7:15 pm.
			Saturday schedule
				Setup: 8:45 to 9:00 am.
				AM block: 9:00 am to 1:00 pm.
				Transition: 1:00 to 2:00 pm (staggered pickup and drop-off; no lunch served).
				PM block: 2:00 to 6:00 pm.
				Reset: 6:00 to 6:15 pm.
			Every campus runs four cohorts a week: Monday/Wednesday, Tuesday/Thursday, Saturday morning, and Saturday afternoon. The same room and the same guide team serve all four, and every cohort gets the same four weekly hours.
		Capacity
			Room capacity is the seats filled at one time, set per campus.
			Enrolled students per campus is four times the room capacity: two weekday tracks plus two Saturday blocks. A 100-seat room fills to 400 enrolled students.
			The operating goal is to fill every cohort to capacity.
		Enrollment
			Five sessions a year, each about seven weeks (about 35 program weeks).
			Families sign up one session at a time, choosing a weekday track or a Saturday block, with a one-session minimum. They re-enroll session to session.
			One session at a time lowers the barrier to trying Alpha and cycles far more distinct families through each seat than an annual plan.
		Real estate
			The building is a sunk cost Alpha already carries, whether it owns the campus or leases it, with after-hours and weekend access either way. Evening and Saturday use adds only marginal cost.
			Marginal cost about $150 to $250 per weekday evening, about $400 per Saturday (custodial, HVAC and lighting, security). Because the host is an Alpha campus, the facility fee is largely intracompany.
		Team
			Head of Alpha Hours (Nancy Wisniewski Torvund): $400,000. Owns strategy, pricing, national rollout, campus relationships, conversion targets, and the full P&L.
			Operations and Admin Manager: $110,000 to $130,000. Enrollment, billing, host-campus contracts, compliance, insurance, payroll, reporting.
			Campus Coordinator: $75,000 to $95,000. Launches sites, trains guides, runs quality, owns the parent experience. One, then more as sites grow.
			Dedicated Alpha Hours guides. Weekday evenings run about 2.5 hours (4:45 to 7:15 with setup and reset), four evenings a week, plus a roughly 10-hour Saturday, about 20 staff-hours a week. Guides can also work Alpha campus afternoon shifts. A blended daytime-plus-evening role is a real full-time job at roughly $50,000 to $85,000 depending on metro, which attracts stronger candidates and cuts turnover.
			Site lead / lead guide: $30 to $55 per hour. Runs the block, owns dismissal safety.
			Staffing: about 1 guide per 10 students, two-adult minimum, extra attention to the youngest. Guides scale with the room, not the cohort count, so the same team serves all four cohorts.
		Compliance
			Design and market as academic instruction, not childcare, to qualify for state exemptions. Avoid meals where they trigger licensing (Florida especially). Sign a formal host-campus facility-use agreement. Get a written exemption determination per state before opening. Every block is a curriculum-driven instructional period; the Saturday four-hour block is structured as two two-hour instructional periods with a supervised break, and no lunch is served. Flag the Saturday format specifically in each state's written exemption request.
			Background checks on every hire: fingerprint criminal check, national sex offender registry, state child-abuse registry. Two adults on site at all times.
			Insurance: general liability, professional liability, workers' compensation, and sexual abuse and molestation coverage (excluded from most general liability policies, must be added).
	Pricing
		Per session, both subjects always included, priced on value not hours.
		Standard markets: $3,500 per session, $17,500 per year. Premium markets: $4,500 per session, $22,500 per year.
		The East Coast pilot prices every campus at the premium tier ($4,500). The standard tier is a flex option for lower-tuition national metros.
		We sell a place, not hours. A session is four hours a week for seven weeks, about 28 hours. At $4,500 that is roughly $161 an hour, just under elite 1:1 tutors ($200 to $300), who cover one subject; we cover two, supervised, with the data to prove progress. A single session is four to five times the priciest mainstream competitor for the same span. The full year is roughly a third (30 to 40 percent) of full Alpha tuition ($40,000 to $75,000). We quote the session and full-year numbers, not the hour.

			Per-campus fixed cost: guides (scale with room capacity, same team all four cohorts), facility about $45,000 a year, insurance about $8,000.
			Margin about 87 percent, because four cohorts run through each room while guide and facility cost stay fixed, and the software is Alpha's own.
